# Release, Deployment, Operations, and Reliability Roles

## Roles

- **Site Reliability Engineer (SRE)**: applies software engineering to operations; owns reliability through SLOs, error budgets, incident response, and eliminating toil. Google's founding definition: "what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations function." [https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/](https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/)
- **DevOps Engineer**: not a job with a fixed practice boundary but "the cultural glue" — CI/CD, automation, and collaboration to break down dev/ops walls; focused on delivery speed. [https://www.splunk.com/en_us/blog/learn/sre-vs-devops-vs-platform-engineering.html](https://www.splunk.com/en_us/blog/learn/sre-vs-devops-vs-platform-engineering.html)
- **Platform Engineer**: builds and operates internal developer platforms and self-service tooling so product teams don't reinvent infrastructure; treated as a product discipline (see Team Topologies below). [https://www.infoworld.com/article/4037775/devops-sre-and-platform-engineering-whats-the-difference.html](https://www.infoworld.com/article/4037775/devops-sre-and-platform-engineering-whats-the-difference.html)
- **Release Manager**: owns the go/no-go process and coordination of a release across stakeholders (business, QA, ops); a cross-functional coordination role, distinct from the engineer who builds release tooling. [unsourced — no primary source found distinguishing "Release Manager" as job title separate from Release Engineer in Google materials]
- **Release Engineer**: Google-defined discipline responsible for source-code management, build config, branching/version management, and release tooling; treated as its own engineering specialty inside SRE-adjacent orgs. [https://sre.google/sre-book/release-engineering/](https://sre.google/sre-book/release-engineering/)
- **Launch Coordination Engineer (LCE, Google)**: an SRE specialization that accelerates product launches while applying SRE judgment to reliability; brings together stakeholders, mediates conflicts, keeps business informed when corners are cut for speed; either hired directly or drawn from SREs with hands-on production experience. [https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/](https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/)
- **Incident Commander (IC)**: owns and leads incident response from escalation to postmortem completion; makes key decisions but does not personally perform remediation; per PagerDuty's model, also authors (or delegates) the initial postmortem template and runs the postmortem meeting. [https://response.pagerduty.com/training/incident_commander/](https://response.pagerduty.com/training/incident_commander/)
- **On-call engineer**: first responder who is paged for alerts; the operational front line whose job is triage, mitigation, and escalation to the IC when needed. [https://www.pagerduty.com/resources/digital-operations/learn/incident-response-lifecycle-for-devops/](https://www.pagerduty.com/resources/digital-operations/learn/incident-response-lifecycle-for-devops/)
- **Production Engineer (Meta)**: Meta's name for its SRE-equivalent role, but deliberately hybrid — writes significant production code (automation, tooling, infra software) and is embedded directly inside product/infra teams rather than sitting in a separate ops org. [https://medium.com/@ashnashahgrover777/production-engineering-versus-software-engineering-5ea0c67e6899](https://medium.com/@ashnashahgrover777/production-engineering-versus-software-engineering-5ea0c67e6899)
- **Infrastructure Engineer**: builds/operates the underlying compute, network, and platform substrate that other roles (SRE, platform, product teams) run on top of; boundary with Platform Engineer is often organizational rather than technical. [unsourced — role commonly used in industry job postings but no canonical definitional source located distinguishing it cleanly from Platform Engineer]

## Responsibility boundaries

- The cleanest three-way split found: **DevOps = mindset/culture** (delivery speed via automation and collaboration), **SRE = practice** (production reliability via SLOs/error budgets/incident response — "ops through the lens of engineering"), **Platform Engineering = product approach** (standardized self-service developer workflows). No single discipline is described as sufficient alone at enterprise scale; they are presented as complementary rather than substitutable. [https://www.coherentsolutions.com/insights/devops-sre-platform-engineering](https://www.coherentsolutions.com/insights/devops-sre-platform-engineering), [https://www.splunk.com/en_us/blog/learn/sre-vs-devops-vs-platform-engineering.html](https://www.splunk.com/en_us/blog/learn/sre-vs-devops-vs-platform-engineering.html)
- SRE-vs-DevOps boundary in practice: SRE is described as "one specific, prescriptive implementation of DevOps," giving DevOps's principles concrete mechanisms (error budgets, blameless postmortems, toil budgets), while generic "DevOps engineer" work tends to be CI/CD pipeline and automation-tooling focused without a formal reliability-target contract. [https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/](https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/)
- Platform Engineering's boundary with both: platform teams exist to reduce cognitive load on stream-aligned (product) teams by packaging infrastructure/tooling as an internally-consumed "product" with its own roadmap, docs, and support — a productization stance neither classic DevOps nor classic SRE explicitly prescribes. [https://teamtopologies.com/videos-slides/what-is-platform-as-a-product-clues-from-team-topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/videos-slides/what-is-platform-as-a-product-clues-from-team-topologies)
- Meta collapses the SRE/build boundary deliberately: Production Engineers are embedded in product teams and write code, rather than being a separate central reliability org — a structural alternative to Google's more centralized SRE model. [https://medium.com/@ashnashahgrover777/production-engineering-versus-software-engineering-5ea0c67e6899](https://medium.com/@ashnashahgrover777/production-engineering-versus-software-engineering-5ea0c67e6899)
- Within Google, LCE is explicitly a specialization of SRE (not a separate discipline): LCEs are held to the same technical bar as any SRE, layering in launch-specific communication/coordination duties. [https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/](https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/)
- Incident Commander vs on-call: IC is a decision-making/coordination role that can be filled by someone other than the paged engineer; the on-call engineer is the initial responder who may hand off command once an incident is declared major enough. [https://response.pagerduty.com/training/incident_commander/](https://response.pagerduty.com/training/incident_commander/)

## Artifacts produced

- **Launch checklist / production readiness review (PRR)**: Google's original Launch Coordination Checklist (circa 2005) — a structured list SREs/LCEs use to verify a service is architecturally and operationally ready before public launch (capacity, monitoring, rollback plan, dependencies). Consumed by LCEs, service SREs, and launch stakeholders as the go/no-go input. [https://sre.google/sre-book/launch-checklist/](https://sre.google/sre-book/launch-checklist/)
- **Runbook**: documented, often automatable remediation steps for known failure modes, used by on-call engineers during an active incident to act quickly under pressure; increasingly encoded as executable workflows rather than static docs. [https://how2.sh/posts/how-to-set-up-incident-containment-runbooks-for-engineering-teams/](https://how2.sh/posts/how-to-set-up-incident-containment-runbooks-for-engineering-teams/)
- **SLO definition document**: states the target reliability level for a service (e.g., 99.9% availability) derived from SLIs; owned nominally by product management setting the expectation, operationalized by SRE. Consumed by both engineering (to prioritize reliability work) and business/product stakeholders (to understand the reliability contract). [https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/](https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/)
- **Error budget policy**: a document specifying what happens when a service's error budget is exhausted — typically: releases proceed normally if the service is within SLO, but launches other than P0/security fixes are halted if the error budget for the trailing window is spent. Consumed by release engineers/managers as a release gate and by leadership as an accountability mechanism. [https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/](https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/)
- **Rollout plan**: describes how a change is staged into production (canary percentages, blue-green cutover steps, feature-flag sequencing) and the metrics/thresholds that trigger promotion or rollback. Consumed by release engineers, SRE/on-call (for monitoring), and the IC if rollback is triggered. [https://www.getunleash.io/blog/canary-release-vs-progressive-delivery](https://www.getunleash.io/blog/canary-release-vs-progressive-delivery)
- **Incident timeline**: a timestamped reconstruction of what happened during an incident, built by the IC/responders during and immediately after the event; the factual backbone of the postmortem. [https://response.pagerduty.com/after/post_mortem_process/](https://response.pagerduty.com/after/post_mortem_process/)
- **Postmortem document**: blameless write-up covering impact, actions taken, root cause(s), and prevention follow-ups; assumes good intent of participants; owned initially by the IC (who creates the template) then handed to an assigned individual owner. Consumed by the engineering team (for follow-up work), management (for accountability of action items), and sometimes customers/public (for transparency). [https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/), [https://response.pagerduty.com/after/post_mortem_process/](https://response.pagerduty.com/after/post_mortem_process/)
- **On-call handoff notes**: [unsourced — widely practiced but no primary/canonical source located describing a standard content contract for on-call handoff notes]
- **Change record**: the artifact historically reviewed by an ITIL Change Advisory Board (CAB) before a production change is approved; in DevOps-native orgs this is increasingly replaced by policy-as-code checks (automated validation of test coverage, security scans) rather than a manual document review. [https://www.harness.io/blog/change-advisory-board-really-needed](https://www.harness.io/blog/change-advisory-board-really-needed)

## Handoff points

- **Development → release**: the most commonly cited breakage is the classic "throw it over the wall" pattern under a separate-ops model — code shipped without operability built in, and ops engineers lacking the knowledge to run it; this is the core justification Amazon and DevOps proponents give for "you build it, you run it." [https://www.infoworld.com/article/2336371/devs-don-t-want-to-do-ops.html](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2336371/devs-don-t-want-to-do-ops.html), [https://stackoverflow.blog/2023/03/20/who-builds-it-and-who-runs-it-sre-team-topologies/](https://stackoverflow.blog/2023/03/20/who-builds-it-and-who-runs-it-sre-team-topologies/)
- **Release → on-call/operations**: Google's error-budget policy is explicitly designed as the handoff contract at this point — a service within its SLO gets normal release velocity, one that has burned its error budget gets releases frozen (except P0/security), forcing dev effort back onto reliability work before further launches are accepted into production. [https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/](https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/)
- **Incident → engineering backlog → prevention work**: the postmortem's action items are the formal handoff mechanism; PagerDuty's model stresses each action item must have a single named individual owner (not a team) or the handoff silently fails and follow-up work never gets done. [https://response.pagerduty.com/after/post_mortem_process/](https://response.pagerduty.com/after/post_mortem_process/)

## Gates and decision criteria

- **Production readiness review (PRR) / launch checklist**: gate before public launch; Google evolved from a late-stage "Simple PRR" applicable only to services already in the launch phase toward an "Early Engagement Model" that pulls SRE review earlier in the development lifecycle to design for reliability rather than retrofit it. [https://sre.google/sre-book/evolving-sre-engagement-model/](https://sre.google/sre-book/evolving-sre-engagement-model/)
- **Go/no-go authority**: at Google, Launch Coordination Engineers hold this authority for major launches — they are empowered to slow or block a launch and are responsible for telling stakeholders when corners are being cut for speed. [https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/](https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/)
- **Canary promotion / rollback criteria**: metric-driven — request error rate, p99 latency, and at least one business metric are monitored against the stable baseline (not absolute thresholds); a common rule cited is rollback if the canary is 50% worse than stable on any tracked metric. [https://www.getunleash.io/blog/canary-release-vs-progressive-delivery](https://www.getunleash.io/blog/canary-release-vs-progressive-delivery)
- **Error budget exhaustion as a release freeze**: the explicit stated policy — if a service has exceeded its error budget over the trailing four-week window, releases other than P0 issues or security fixes are halted until it's back within SLO. This is an automatic, metric-triggered gate rather than a discretionary committee decision. [https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/](https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/)
- **CAB (ITIL) vs automated gates**: research is cited showing CAB/external-approval processes correlate negatively with lead time, deployment frequency, and MTTR, with no correlation to change failure rate — i.e., the manual committee gate does not measurably improve safety while it measurably slows delivery. ITIL 4 itself moved away from mandatory CAB review of every change, introducing a decentralized "Change Authority" so low-risk/standard changes can be auto-approved and only higher-risk changes route to a CAB-like body. [https://www.harness.io/blog/change-advisory-board-really-needed](https://www.harness.io/blog/change-advisory-board-really-needed), [https://itsm.tools/change-enablement/](https://itsm.tools/change-enablement/)
- **Incident rollback trigger / stop authority**: the Incident Commander is the decision-maker during an active incident (including whether to roll back), explicitly not the one executing the remediation personally — decision and execution are split by design. [https://response.pagerduty.com/training/incident_commander/](https://response.pagerduty.com/training/incident_commander/)

## Practice variation and open debates

- **You-build-it-you-run-it vs separate ops**: Amazon's CTO's dictum ties incentive to consequence (on-call developers are motivated to build reliable systems); critics note it isn't universally applicable and some organizations retain a real role for a separate ops function, splitting responsibility across the lifecycle instead of a binary choice. [https://www.infoworld.com/article/2336371/devs-don-t-want-to-do-ops.html](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2336371/devs-don-t-want-to-do-ops.html), [https://www.equalexperts.com/blog/our-thinking/how-to-decide-when-to-use-you-build-it-you-run-it-and-when-not-to-use-it/](https://www.equalexperts.com/blog/our-thinking/how-to-decide-when-to-use-you-build-it-you-run-it-and-when-not-to-use-it/)
- **Is "DevOps engineer" a contradiction?**: sources frame DevOps as a cultural/mindset practice rather than a discrete job function, which is in tension with the industry's widespread use of "DevOps Engineer" as an actual job title; the disambiguation offered is that SRE is what happens when the DevOps philosophy gets a prescriptive, measurable implementation (error budgets, SLOs), implying "DevOps engineer" job titles often really do SRE- or platform-shaped work under a different name. [https://www.splunk.com/en_us/blog/learn/sre-vs-devops-vs-platform-engineering.html](https://www.splunk.com/en_us/blog/learn/sre-vs-devops-vs-platform-engineering.html)
- **SRE as distinct org vs embedded**: Google's model centralizes SRE as a distinct organization that engages with product teams (including a formal engagement-model evolution from late-stage PRR to early design involvement); Meta's Production Engineer model instead embeds the equivalent function directly inside product/infra teams from the start. Both are presented as legitimate, differently-structured answers to the same reliability-ownership question. [https://sre.google/sre-book/evolving-sre-engagement-model/](https://sre.google/sre-book/evolving-sre-engagement-model/), [https://medium.com/@ashnashahgrover777/production-engineering-versus-software-engineering-5ea0c67e6899](https://medium.com/@ashnashahgrover777/production-engineering-versus-software-engineering-5ea0c67e6899)
- **ITIL/CAB vs continuous deployment**: the debate is empirically informed rather than purely philosophical — the multi-year DORA-adjacent study cited above found CAB/external-approval hurts delivery-speed metrics with no offsetting safety benefit, pushing ITIL 4 itself toward decentralized, risk-tiered change authority and policy-as-code automated gates as the reconciliation path. [https://www.harness.io/blog/change-advisory-board-really-needed](https://www.harness.io/blog/change-advisory-board-really-needed)
- **Platform team as product**: Team Topologies' prescription is that platform teams must operate with product-management discipline (roadmap, internal customer focus, "Thinnest Viable Platform") rather than as a traditional shared-infrastructure/ops team — case studies cited include Adidas and Uswitch adopting this model to reduce stream-aligned team cognitive load. [https://teamtopologies.com/videos-slides/what-is-platform-as-a-product-clues-from-team-topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/videos-slides/what-is-platform-as-a-product-clues-from-team-topologies)
- **DORA elite-vs-low performer spread**: cited as evidence for why these debates matter operationally — elite performers deploy on demand with lead time under an hour/day and change failure rates near 5%, while low performers take one to six months per change, illustrating the throughput/stability tradeoff DORA metrics are designed to jointly track (deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore). [https://octopus.com/devops/metrics/dora-metrics/](https://octopus.com/devops/metrics/dora-metrics/), [https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/using-the-four-keys-to-measure-your-devops-performance](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/using-the-four-keys-to-measure-your-devops-performance)
